Historical Context

The Chronology of Water and Survival

1998

The Long Deluge: Bangladesh Under Water

The 1998 flood is widely regarded as one of the most prolonged and devastating natural disasters in modern Bangladeshi history. Lasting for over two agonizing months, the floodwaters submerged nearly 68% of the country, directly affecting over 30 million people. The catastrophe was triggered by the synchronization of peak flows in the main river systems—the Ganges, Brahmaputra, and Meghna—combined with heavy monsoon rains.

Unlike flash floods, the water in 1998 refused to recede. Capital city Dhaka and vast rural expanses were paralyzed for weeks, destroying millions of homes, wiping out standing crops, and shutting down the country's infrastructure.

The 1998 Experience: "A Test of Endurance"

"We lived on the rooftop under a makeshift bamboo shed for almost six weeks. The ground below was just an endless sea of dark, stagnant water. Dhaka was quiet except for the sound of relief helicopters and small country boats moving between power poles. There was no electricity, and the smell of contaminated water was everywhere. We didn't battle a sudden rush of water; we battled the exhaustion of watching a flood that refused to leave."

2004

The Urban Disruption: Submerging the Capital

In July 2004, heavy monsoon rains coupled with excessive cross-border river flow flooded about 38% of Bangladesh. This flood particularly crippled the mega-city of Dhaka and its surrounding industrial belts. Over 30 million people nationwide were affected as critical sewage systems collapsed, mixing floodwaters with waste and sparking massive clean-water crises across both rural and urban areas.

The 2004 Experience: "Dhaka in Boats"

"I remember major diplomatic zones and posh areas of Dhaka being completely cut off. People were using traditional wooden boats to buy groceries on main city avenues. The basement parking lots of high-rises turned into swimming pools, and the ground-floor residents lost everything to dirty sewage-mixed water."

2007

The Double Monsoonal Blow & Cyclone Sidr

The year 2007 was uniquely tragic due to consecutive waves of intense flooding followed shortly by the catastrophic Cyclone Sidr. The initial floods hit in two distinct monsoonal waves, covering over 40% of the country and ruining immense stretches of paddy fields right before harvest season, leading to severe food security threats across northern and central Bangladesh.

The 2007 Experience: "Double Tragedy"

"We had barely cleaned the mud out of our home from the August flood when the second wave hit us in September. Crop fields were washed out twice in a row. Farmers lost all their capital, and just as we were attempting to recover, Cyclone Sidr arrived later that year, leaving us broken."

2022

The Sylhet-Sunamganj Flash Catastrophe

In June 2022, the northeastern region of Bangladesh witnessed its worst flood in 120 years. Record-breaking rainfall in the upstream Indian hills of Meghalaya and Assam brought a massive wall of water crashing down into the Surma and Kushiyara river basins. Within days, Sunamganj and Sylhet were almost entirely isolated from the rest of the country, with over 80% of the division underwater.

The 2022 Experience: "The Fast Drowning of the Northeast"

"The water rushed in like an angry mountain river. Our entire neighborhood in Sunamganj vanished beneath the waves in less than 24 hours. The local airport shut down, train tracks drowned, and for three days, there was absolutely no network or way to tell our relatives that we were hiding out on the upper floors of a local school."

2024

The Unprecedented Flash Flood: The Drowning of Feni & Noakhali

In August 2024, eastern Bangladesh—specifically Feni, Noakhali, and Comilla—was struck with terrifying, unprecedented speed. Triggered by torrential upstream rainfall and the sudden release of water from transboundary river systems, the Muhuri and Feni rivers breached their banks within hours.

This region, historically unaccustomed to severe flooding, was completely caught off guard. Power grids collapsed instantly, and telecommunication networks went entirely silent for days, plunging millions into total isolation and darkness. The disaster sparked a historic, student-and-citizen-led nationwide rescue mobilization.

The 2024 Experience: "The Silent, Sudden Terror"

"In 2024, there was no time to prepare. The water didn't rise over weeks; it came up to our necks in a matter of three to four hours in the dead of night. The most horrifying part was the absolute silence that followed—no phone signals, no electricity, just the roaring current outside. Those five days of silence felt like an eternity for families stranded on roofs and for our loved ones abroad who thought we were already gone."
